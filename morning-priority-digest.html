Morning Priority Digest

I automated the daily triage of support email and ClickUp hand-offs into one ranked Slack brief, so the team starts the day on the messages that matter most instead of hunting through two inboxes.

My role
Product lead, end to end
Team
Operations, Support, Product
Sources
Gmail, ClickUp, Stripe receipts
Delivery
Slack, every morning
Status
Live, in test window

The problem

Customer messages arrived by email across several inboxes. Some needed a reply within hours, such as a lapsed subscriber or a billing error. Others were routine, like a receipt or a calendar cancellation. Team members spent the first part of each morning sorting the pile before doing any real work, and urgent items could sit unseen under routine ones.

Hand-offs were also scattered. When a message needed a person to act, it was moved into a ClickUp list by hand, and nothing showed the whole picture in one place.

The goal: one brief each morning that answers "what do I handle first today?" in under five minutes, with nothing urgent buried.

What the team sees each morning

The digest posts to a dedicated Slack channel. Each item shows when it arrived, who it came from, a one-line subject, and a short preview. Below is a sample built from invented example data.

How it works

1. Collect

Pull new messages from the support inbox and transactional senders, and read open ClickUp hand-offs. Duplicates and self-sent test mail are removed first.

2. Rank

Sort each item into one of three tiers. P1 is needs action now, P2 is needs a reply soon, and P3 is for information only. New accounts are shown in their own section.

3. Deliver

Post a single message in a fixed order, so the team learns the layout and reads it the same way every day.

4. Check

A test window ran the digest against a rolling 25-day history before it went live, so the ranking could be checked against real examples.

To confirm before publishing: the exact tools used for the classifier and the scheduling method, and the specific rules for each tier. This was written from the digest output alone.

Key product decisions

Ranking by action needed, not by sender

I grouped items by what the reader must do next, not by which inbox they came from. A payment failure from a billing sender is more urgent than a newsletter from a known contact, and the layout shows that at a glance.

A fixed format with three tiers

Three tiers are enough to separate "act now" from "reply today" from "good to know". More tiers would have made the brief harder to scan, and it would have been unclear which items deserved attention first.

Keeping the human in charge

The digest only ranks and summarises. It never sends a reply or changes a ClickUp status, so the team stays in control of every customer conversation.

Testing on real history before going live

Running the digest against 25 days of past mail let me see where a message landed and correct the rules before anyone relied on it.

What I would do next

  • Add feedback buttons in Slack (useful / wrong tier) to measure ranking accuracy over time.
  • Learn from the feedback so repeat senders and recurring issues rank correctly without manual rules.
  • Add a weekly summary of volume by tier to spot where the support load is growing.

What I learned

Most of the value came from the format, not the technology. The ranking rules mattered, but a fixed layout that the team reads the same way every morning mattered more. Testing against real history found more problems than any design review would have.

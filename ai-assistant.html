Finding and Fixing the Friction in an AI Assistant

The AI assistant is member-facing and recommends resources to parents and professionals. I analysed real chat logs, found the recurring failures, changed the prompt and routing rules, and tested each change with a written case set before release.

My role
Product lead for AI assistant behaviour
Platform
Chatbase, no-code AI assistant
Partners
Support, Content, Dev team
Method
Log review, prompt tests, release
Status
Pushed after test rounds

The problem

The assistant launched as a limited first version with a parent-coach framing. Over time it was being used for more than resource search. Members asked about access, cancellations, downloads and kits, and first-time visitors used it as a starting point. The team needed the assistant to answer well for both parents and professionals, without the cost of a custom build.

Chat logs showed the same few failure modes again and again. Each one hurt the member experience in a way a single fix could address.

What I found in the logs

Failure patternWhat members experiencedFix I tried
Loops on follow-upA reply with two questions in it made a simple "yes" ambiguous, so the assistant asked again.Single-question replies and an explicit yes/no branch.
Support questions in a resource botAccount, payment and cancellation questions took several turns before the assistant routed them to support.An early routing rule for account keywords.
Kit links not returnedRequests for a named kit were deflected, even though the links existed.A keyword-to-link map in the prompt, with a clear limit on how far it could scale.
Age asked repeatedlyA parent gave a child's age, then was asked again on each topic change.Logged as a product gap. Not solvable within the prompt.
Parent framing for professionalsPhrases like "how old is your child" assumed a parent, which confused educators and therapists.Reworded prompt language and removed child-specific questions for professional users.

Pattern I noticed: adding more rules to one prompt sometimes made answers less reliable, so each change needed a regression check, not just a check of the new behaviour.

My approach

1. Read the logs before changing anything

I reviewed real conversations and grouped them by the reason a member got stuck. That kept the work tied to what members actually asked, not to what the team assumed they would ask.

2. Rewrite the smallest thing that could work

Where a fix was possible through the prompt, I changed the prompt. Where it was not, I logged it as a product gap with the evidence attached.

3. Test against a fixed case set

Every change was checked against the same set of written cases, so a fix for one pattern could not quietly break another.

4. Release after sign-off, then watch

I pushed the updated version only after several test rounds and sign-off from the operations lead, then kept an eye on the next round of logs.

How I wrote the test cases

Each case has an intent, a realistic member message, the expected behaviour, and a pass or fail result. Cases cover the common flows and the known failure patterns above.

Case: Parent asks for a named kit by name Input: "Where can I find the screen time toolkit?" Expected: Returns that kit's link, no age question Check: Pass / Fail, with the reply pasted in
Case: Account question in a resource bot Input: "I can't cancel my subscription" Expected: Routes to the support team in the first reply Check: Pass / Fail
Case: Professional user, no child mentioned Input: "I'm a school counsellor looking for activities for my class" Expected: Resource-focused reply, no assumption of a parent

Key decisions

Prompt fixes first, custom build later

I tested prompt changes before asking for a custom build. Some problems improved, and others, like remembering a child's age across a session, could not be fixed in the prompt. That gave the team concrete evidence for the next decision.

Route early, don't keep the member guessing

Account and billing questions go to a human as early as possible. A resource bot should not try to solve those itself.

Keep the testing and the decision separate

I could say what the prompt could and could not do. The choice of whether to invest in a larger build was for the leadership team, so I presented the trade-offs and the evidence without making that call.

The clearest outcome was a better-defined boundary. Some problems were fixed in the prompt, and others were documented as needing product changes, so the next investment could be made on evidence.

What I learned

  • An AI assistant is a product with a limited surface. Before adding rules, test what the surface can actually support.
  • Written test cases matter more than a quick check. They catch regressions that a spot check misses.
  • Support routing is a product decision as much as a prompt decision. Members feel it immediately.
